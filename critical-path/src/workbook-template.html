Critical Path Analysis: why should you care?

Your NEA hand-in is coming. Your components take a week to arrive. The 3D printer has a queue. Spray paint needs a day to dry. You still have to test with your client and write the evaluation. Which jobs actually decide whether you finish on time?

That is the only question Critical Path Analysis (CPA) answers. Manufacturers use it to launch products on time, and it comes up in Paper 1. It is also exactly what you need right now: a realistic plan for finishing your prototype and folder (Grid 6 asks for one).

By the end of this workbook you can:
  • use the key words: activity, dependency, network, node, EST, LFT, float, critical path
  • draw and complete a network diagram, and find the critical path and the float
  • predict what happens when something is delayed
  • use CPA to plan the rest of your own NEA, and answer exam questions on it

Page 3 · Worked example

Finishing a Year 13 prototype

Every NEA is different, but the last weeks of most look like this: order parts, make, finish, assemble, test with the client, and write it up. Here is one student's plan for a desk lamp with a CNC-cut body, 3D-printed parts and bought-in electronics. Durations are in school days.

1Spot the parallel jobs

2Forward pass: fill in the ESTs

Start at node 1 with EST 0. Move left to right. For each node: EST = the EST of the node before + the activity's duration. If two arrows meet, take the bigger number (you cannot start until everything feeding in is finished).

3Backward pass: fill in the LFTs

Start at the last node: its LFT = its EST. Move right to left. For each node: LFT = the LFT of the next node − the activity's duration. If two arrows leave a node, take the smaller number.

4Find the critical path

On the critical path, EST = LFT at every node and there is no slack. Tap the activity labels on the diagram that are on the critical path, then check.

Careful: an activity can join two nodes where EST = LFT and still not be critical. It is only critical if EST at its start + its duration = LFT at its end.

5Float

Float = LFT at the end node − EST at the start node − duration. It is how many days an activity can slip without delaying the project.

Stuck? Re-read the rule above each step: bigger number going forwards, smaller going backwards.

Plan the rest of your own NEA

Everyone's project is different, so now use CPA on yours. List every job left between now and hand-in: making, finishing, ordering, waiting, testing with your client and writing up the folder pages. Give each a letter, a duration and the letters it depends on. The workbook works out your critical path, floats and Gantt chart.

Tips. Split big jobs ("CNC the base", not "make the base"). Include waiting time: deliveries, glue curing, paint drying, printer queues. Include the folder pages, client testing and the evaluation. Be honest about durations: the plan is only as good as your estimates. Aim for 8 to 16 activities.

Page 7 · Evaluate it

Is CPA worth it?

1Advantage or disadvantage?

2CPA and the other project management methods

Gantt chart

A bar chart of tasks against time. Easy to read and great for showing a plan to others, but it does not show dependencies as clearly as a network.

Scrum

A team-based, goal-driven, iterative and agile approach: short "sprints" with regular reviews. Suits design work where the plan changes as you learn.

Six Sigma

A short-term improvement project using DMAIC: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. Aims to cut defects and variation.

Page 10 · Summary

What you now know

The method
  1. List activities, durations, dependencies.
  2. Draw the network.
  3. Forward pass: ESTs (take the bigger number where arrows meet).
  4. Backward pass: LFTs (take the smaller number where arrows split).
  5. Critical path: nodes with EST = LFT, activities with zero float.
  6. Float = LFT(end) − EST(start) − duration.
Why it matters
  • Shows the shortest possible project time.
  • Shows which delays matter (critical) and which do not (float).
  • Tells you where to put extra time, people or money.
  • Only as good as the duration estimates.
